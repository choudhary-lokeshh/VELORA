# Production media freeze report

## What this records

The production media platform's core is frozen: the internal architecture, its invariants, and the code that carries them are complete and green, and no further work on them is planned until something below unfreezes them.

Freezing the core is not the same as being able to store, scan, or serve a single byte in production. This document exists mostly to keep those apart, in the same way the [monetization freeze report](11-monetization-freeze-report.md) separates a frozen money architecture from an ability to charge anybody and the [trust and safety freeze report](12-trust-safety-freeze-report.md) separates frozen enforcement machinery from an ability to enforce. Velora can take an upload, refuse it, derive what it actually is, produce sanitized derivatives, decide who may see them, take them down, and reconcile the result against a provider — every path built and tested against a filesystem adapter. It cannot accept production media, because **no object-storage provider, no delivery provider, and no malware scanner is approved**, and each of those ports defaults to a value that refuses everything. Those are decisions nobody has made, not components nobody has built.

## What is frozen

The asset and its technical lifecycle, which deliberately shares no value with any publication vocabulary; server-generated opaque object keys and narrow, single-object, expiring upload capabilities; upload windows that may be replaced but never reused; inspection that derives format, decodability, dimensions, pixel count, frame count, digest, and metadata presence from the stored bytes rather than from anything a client said; quarantine as terminal for delivery; the malware scanner seam as a claim separate from decoding; derivatives rendered from decoded pixels under a recorded processing version; the publication bridge that composes technical readiness with the owning domain's association, the viewer's entitlement, and Trust and Safety's current answer; public immutable delivery and private credential-bound delivery as two paths that cannot be confused; consumer profile media, creator avatars and covers, and private club content attachments; takedown, provider deletion, cache purge as separately recorded work, and legal hold; reconciliation on a rolling cursor with exactly three permitted corrections and a drift vocabulary for the disagreements no correction is safe for; the operator read and the single purge action; and the partial indexes that keep every one of those reads proportional to what is outstanding rather than to what has ever been done.

Eight migrations carry it, `0038_media_platform` through `0045_quarantined_removal` — two of them, `0040_users_profile_media_assets` and `0041_creator_media_attachments`, landing in the owning domains where the associations live rather than here. Five tables are owned by MEDIA, all prefixed `media_`: assets, upload sessions, objects, obligations, and drift findings. No other domain writes one and no other domain reads one; the coarse readiness projection exists precisely so the technical lifecycle cannot be learned by accident, and it is a permitted non-authoritative projection that delivery never consults.

MEDIA publishes no HTTP route, and that is a decision rather than an omission: an upload endpoint belonging to no product domain would be a purpose-free upload endpoint, and somebody could reserve storage with no product reason at all. A test asserts the route registry contains no media path, so one cannot appear quietly.

## What is not frozen, and why

**LIVE PRODUCTION MEDIA IS BLOCKED.** Not by an incomplete implementation but by ports with no approved value behind them and decisions nobody has made:

- **No object-storage provider.** `MEDIA_STORAGE_PROVIDER` defaults to `unavailable`, which refuses every operation, and staging and production reject any other value. Primary-source research recorded in [media provider eligibility](../compliance/08-media-provider-eligibility.md) found S3, Google Cloud Storage, R2, Backblaze B2, and DigitalOcean Spaces silent on lawful adult content — the absence of an answer rather than approval — bunny.net the only assessed provider permitting it in writing, and Azure and Wasabi unassessed because no governing policy document was retrievable.
- **No delivery or CDN provider.** `MEDIA_DELIVERY_PROVIDER` defaults to `unavailable`. The Cloudflare restriction on serving a disproportionate percentage of pictures without a named paid service is a design input rather than a footnote, and AWS documents no mechanism for revoking an individual presigned URL. Purge semantics and latency are questions asked of a candidate, not assumed of one.
- **No malware or content scanning position.** `MEDIA_MALWARE_SCANNER` defaults to `unavailable`, which refuses, and a refusal quarantines rather than passing — so an environment with no scanning position accepts no media at all. No scanner was assessed, because a scanner is only reachable once bytes are somewhere to scan. **The absence of a decision is not a decision that scanning is unnecessary.**
- **No retention durations.** How long a quarantined original, a deleted asset's evidence, and an object under a Safety legal hold are kept is `LEGAL REVIEW REQUIRED`. No duration is invented in code, nothing expires on a timer, and no correctness rule depends on an object being physically gone — so an approved schedule later applies as a deletion pass rather than as a redesign. The twenty-four-hour technical TTL that reclaims never-completed upload windows is a resource policy about objects nobody finished uploading and is deliberately not a precedent for one.
- **No admin sign-in implementation.** Every Platform Admin route requires the Platform Admin audience and a fresh phishing-resistant assurance, and no approved verifier produces one. **The operator surface is unreachable in every environment**, and [media operations](../operations/06-media-operations.md) says so in its opening rather than reading as a live procedure.
- **No legal-hold authority.** `setLegalHold` exists and the database refuses to record a held asset as `deleted`; no route exposes it. The mechanism is complete and the authority is not, and an operator placing a hold with no enforcement record behind it would be an unaudited action on evidence.
- **No alert routing or on-call ownership.** Every backlog class publishes the threshold the platform's own sweeps run on, so an alert rule need not invent one — and nothing routes any of them to anybody.

Image processing is the one seam with no provider question attached, and that is worth stating so it is not mistaken for an open gate: it runs in-process on sharp over libvips, which is a dependency decision under [dependency risk acceptance](../security/08-dependency-risk-acceptance.md) rather than a provider decision, because no bytes leave the machine and there is no account to be approved. It was forced rather than preferred — Cloudinary, imgix, and Mux each prohibit material on terms reaching content Velora does not author.

Two scope facts, recorded so nothing is inferred from the model's generality. **Images only**: the asset and variant model is shaped so a future video class is a new class rather than a new schema, and no transcoding, streaming, or segmented delivery exists or may be assumed. **Remote media import stays disabled**: MEDIA has no path that fetches a caller-supplied address, so enabling import is a new capability with its own egress review rather than a flag.

Not built, and recorded as not built rather than left to be discovered: Creator Studio media management surfaces; rendering of a creator's avatar and cover on the public page, whose association, publication rule, and delivery decision exist and are tested while no client asks for an address; and peer delivery of consumer profile images, because whether one person may see another's profile image is a question about the relationship between two accounts that DISCOVERY owns, and answering it in USERS would have meant inventing the rule.

**MATURE CONTENT PRODUCTION ENABLEMENT REMAINS BLOCKED**, on its own gates and untouched by this milestone. A content attachment declares itself content-gated, which additionally requires classification, depicted-person consent, surface eligibility, viewer assurance, and the mature-content capability; that capability is a single-value enum with no enabling value, no Identity provider or jurisdiction policy is approved for depicted-person evidence, no consent wording is published, and the classification vocabulary is `v1-provisional`. Declaring an item mature enables nothing — it makes the item refusable for a reason rather than for a missing declaration, and a test declares one and watches delivery refuse it. The delivery adapter deliberately passes no viewer adult assurance, because assurance is consulted only for a mature class and a mature class is refused before the question is reached, so supplying a value there could only ever weaken a gate. The [trust and safety freeze report](12-trust-safety-freeze-report.md) is the authority for that block.

## The invariants, and where each is enforced

**An uploaded file is hostile until the platform has proven otherwise from the bytes themselves, and proving what it is does not prove it may be shown.** Every invariant below is a consequence of that sentence.

Nothing a client says is believed. Declared MIME type, filename, extension, dimensions, size, and the client's own claim that an upload succeeded are hints; completion is verified with the provider before `uploaded` is recorded. Object keys are server-generated and opaque, so path traversal is not defended against — it has no input to work with. A window may be replaced but never reused, and a reissue always allocates a fresh key, so bytes arriving under a lapsed capability describe nothing and are destroyed rather than adopted.

Format admission runs **before** the decoder and is a platform allow-list applied to sniffed bytes. libvips reads an SVG document and reports a sensible 64×64 image, which is measured in the suite rather than assumed; an allow-list meaning "whatever the decoder accepts" would therefore admit a script-capable XML dialect on a social platform. JPEG, PNG, and still WebP are admitted, and there is no deny-list beside the list that a case could be added to by mistake. Header and decoder disagreeing is the polyglot signal and is refused. The header is read with the pixel limit off, deliberately, so a bomb is refused with a precise reason rather than recorded as merely undecodable.

Scanning is a separate claim from decoding and the two are never conflated. An unavailable scanner reporting `clean` would be the single most dangerous line in this domain, and there is no configuration under which it can.

Privacy is structural rather than a stripping step somebody could forget. A derivative is rendered from decoded pixels and never assembled by copying the source container, so no EXIF, GPS, device identity, colour profile, or embedded comment has a path to an output. The tests prove the source really carries those things before asserting their absence, and assert the absence of the device string anywhere in the output bytes rather than only the absence of an EXIF block. Orientation is baked in during decode, because a platform that strips every tag and had not rotated first would render every camera photograph sideways for ever.

The database holds the shape of each state and code holds the order of them, and neither suffices alone: the transition map is what stops `quarantined` becoming `ready`, and the constraints are what stop `ready` existing without the measurements that justify it. A row that lies is refused by PostgreSQL rather than found in a log afterwards.

Every effect is idempotent and every obligation durable. PostgreSQL holds the duty, a claim is a database lease rather than a memory fact, and BullMQ is a wake-up rather than a record — so no inspection, processing, deletion, purge, or reconciliation duty is lost because a process died or because the queue was flushed. Concurrency is settled by partial unique indexes rather than by read-then-write, including two rather than one over a nullable column, because a unique index treats nulls as distinct and would admit a duplicate exactly where a duplicate means discharging a deletion twice.

The record is written before the bytes, on both the upload and the processing paths and for the same reason: a crash leaves a record of an object that is missing — which reconciliation can see — rather than bytes at a key nothing references, which nobody would ever look for.

Revocation has two halves and both are always stated together. New authorizations stop the instant any authority changes its answer, because every issuance re-derives the whole decision inside the caller's transaction; already-issued credentials remain valid until they expire, for at most **300 seconds**. That number is reported on every grant as `maximumRevocationExposureSeconds`, and **any claim that media access was revoked instantly which does not name it is false**. For a public derivative the second half is not a TTL but a cache purge, whose semantics belong to a provider and are recorded rather than assumed — `unsupported` is a real outcome, because a provider with no purge mechanism has genuinely not purged and recording that as success would be the platform lying to its own operators about the exposure.

Provider state is evidence, never authority over product or safety state. Exactly three reconciliation corrections exist — destroy bytes nothing claims, restore a derivative from an original the platform still has, and owe the ordinary pipeline a duty it will discharge under its own rules — and there is deliberately no correction that writes a product conclusion. A derivative is never rebuilt for an asset being removed or refused, and never at a processing version other than the one on its row. An asset under legal hold sitting in `deleting` is not a stall; it is doing exactly what a hold means.

Nothing sensitive is logged or published: no raw media byte, signed delivery credential, upload token, provider secret, or EXIF value reaches a log line, and storage keys, digests, byte sizes, and adapter names appear in no product contract. The single exception is inherent rather than chosen — a direct-to-storage upload capability is an address for one object, so that object's key appears inside that URL and nowhere else. On the operator surface the key is deliberately visible, because a key is not a credential and an operator has to be able to name an object to their provider.

## What the hardening phases found

Recorded because the value of an audit is in what it caught:

- **Lifting a legal hold left the deletion owed with nothing to carry it out.** The obligation that ran under the hold had been discharged — it did everything it was permitted to do — so the asset would have sat in `deleting` until a reconciliation pass happened to notice. A duty that depends on somebody spotting it later is not a duty the platform owes. Lifting a hold now owes the deletion again.
- **A worker dying mid-inspection stranded the asset for ever.** It took the `inspecting` state, died before concluding, and its lease expired; the reclaiming worker saw an asset no longer `uploaded`, concluded the work no longer existed, and discharged the duty. Inspection now accepts `inspecting` as well as `uploaded`, the way processing already accepted `processing`, and for the same reason. Reconciliation is the second net rather than the only one.
- **A quarantined asset could never be removed.** The constraint tying `quarantined` to carrying a rejection reason was an equivalence rather than an implication, so moving off `quarantined` while keeping the reason was a state the database refused — and the transition table permits exactly that move. Somebody deleting a rejected upload got a constraint violation; a worker got an obligation that retried and dead-lettered, leaving the removal owed for ever. The constraint one line below it already had the right shape, and the same reasoning simply had not been applied.
- **Two storage methods threw where every other rejects.** Not a live bug, because both callers wrap them in a `try`. It is still an interface that behaves two ways on its error path, which is the sort of thing a future caller handles wrongly exactly once.
- **The upload expiry sweep closed the same rows twice.** Two sweeps took the same identifiers, the second waited on the first's locks, and both reported having closed them — measured at three rows each before the state predicate was moved into the outer statement, so the guarantee no longer rests on skipping alone.
- **A readiness index could not serve the query whose comment claimed it did.** The sweep orders `asc nulls first` so a never-checked slot is picked up before a stale one; a b-tree ASC index stores nulls last, so the planner answered every cycle with a sequential scan and a sort of every attached slot. It is now declared `nulls first`, and the assertion holding it there disables both sequential and bitmap scans so it discriminates at any volume — verified by putting the old index back and watching it fail.
- **Six reads would have become an outage as the tables grew**, each found by measuring the plan the planner actually chose rather than by reading the code. Measured on the real schema at four hundred thousand assets, two hundred thousand upload windows, two hundred thousand objects, and two hundred thousand obligations with `EXPLAIN (ANALYZE, BUFFERS)`: reconciliation's stall query fell from 10,440 buffers — a parallel scan of every asset, every sixty seconds — to 6; the abandonment sweep from 5,506 to 400; recovery of stranded upload windows from a scan and a sort to an index scan; and the three operator aggregates from 10,450, 7,424, and 3,472 to 349, 177, and 177.

Three lessons from how those were fixed are worth as much as the fixes. **A rewrite that reads better can change nothing** — turning the stall query's `not in (select ...)` into a correlated anti-join moved it from 10,440 buffers to 10,434, because the driving scan was still sequential; it needed a narrow index leading on the lifecycle as well, and shipping either half alone would have been a fix that fixed nothing. **Wider is not safer** — a composite index fixes the stall query just as well, is seven times the size, and is then declined by the planner for the operator aggregate for being wider than the question. **A refusal on reasoning was overruled by measurement** — the covering indexes were declined once as a tax on every write for a screen nobody reads continuously, and measured they are 2,784 kB, 1,416 kB, and 1,416 kB against an 82 MB table, buying twenty to thirty times.

Neither red-team finding was reachable by a behaviour test: every test that reaches `deleting` starts from a healthy asset and every test that reaches `quarantined` stops there. Each vocabulary was correct on its own and the *pair* was not, which is the class of defect only an adversarial read across a finished system turns up.

## The evidence this rests on

Twenty consecutive runs of the API integration suite, on the frozen tree, with no retries and no reruns: **1,004 tests across 53 files, 1,004 passing and none failing in every one of the twenty.** Every run was a complete suite against commit `9eb5ffd` with a clean worktree verified before it started, and the runs took between 152 and 192 seconds. The sequence was the whole proof — a suite that passes on a second attempt has been shown to be flaky rather than stable, so any failure would have voided it and restarted it from the first run after a root-cause fix. Above it, 265 unit tests across 20 files.

Inside every one of those twenty, the pool-hardening suite reads `pg_stat_database.deadlocks` before and after each contention scenario and asserts it has not moved, and asserts the pool came back intact with no backend left `idle in transaction` — the failure mode that stalls rather than fails, and therefore the one worth asserting against rather than waiting to notice. Across twenty runs no measured scenario moved it. The one place a deadlock is expected is the harness's own inter-test `TRUNCATE`, which takes an `ACCESS EXCLUSIVE` lock on every table while a read from the previous test may still hold a share lock; it is retried exactly once, on PostgreSQL's own contract, and bounded to one attempt deliberately — a second would mean something genuinely concurrent was running against a database a test believes it owns, and that is a failure rather than a retry. That is a property of the harness, not of the code beneath it.

The fleet property is proved rather than implied. Single-worker suites show one worker behaves and that a second cannot take a leased row; the media concurrency suite shows eight separate runtimes over one database discharge every duty exactly once and lose none — twelve uploads reach `ready` with thirty-six derivatives and not one more, eight deletions are counted across the fleet rather than by one worker so two workers both believing they deleted the same asset would read nine, eight simultaneous reconciliation cycles file one finding and perform one repair, and a duty held by a worker that died is reclaimed by exactly one of the eight once its lease expires on a database instant rather than in the dead process's memory. There are no sleeps in that file: every ordering that matters is a database fact, because a test that waits is a test that passes until the machine is busy, which is when concurrency defects surface.

The lifecycle invariants, the deletion and purge and legal-hold behaviour, and the drift and reconciliation behaviour are carried by named suites inside that same twenty — foundation, upload, inspection, processing, publication, delivery, consumer and creator integration, takedown, reconciliation, scale, the admin operator surface, and the adversarial [`media-red-team`](../../apps/api/test/integration/media-red-team.test.ts) suite the [media threat model](../security/10-media-threat-model.md) records what it attacks and what held.

Five consecutive runs each of the surface suites on the same tree, every run passing: the API unit suite carrying the media policy and runbook guards at 265, Consumer Web at 51, Consumer Mobile at 32, Creator Studio at 38, Platform Admin at 10, the consumer and creator API clients at 10 each, and the browser end-to-end suite at 65 passing across three engines, with 19 skipped for a pre-existing and structural reason rather than a convenient one — a browser that requires HTTPS before it will keep the session cookie cannot run a signed-in journey against a plain-HTTP local server, and the skip names that condition rather than the test. The creator public page, private club media, and safety takedown paths are integration suites and were therefore exercised twenty times rather than five.

Beneath all of it, the canonical `pnpm ci:verify` graph: toolchain and frozen-install verification, workspace policy, formatting, lint, domain boundaries, typecheck, contract generation checked against the committed OpenAPI document and generated client, the authorization policy check, unit tests, the integration suite against real PostgreSQL and Redis, an uncached build of every app, browser end-to-end tests, the mobile doctor, compose validation, secret scanning, whitespace and hygiene, and the dependency security gate. Green end to end on a fresh run over the frozen tree, and green on the hosted pipeline for every commit in this milestone at its exact SHA.

No test was weakened, skipped, or retried to reach any of this, and no media, privacy, or safety control was relaxed to make one pass. Two gate steps went red during freeze clearance — a trailing blank line at end of file, and a `string | undefined` the typechecker was right to reject in a new assertion — and each was fixed and the whole gate re-run from the beginning rather than the failing step re-run on its own.

## What unfreezes this

Any of: an object-storage provider becomes eligible and is selected; a delivery provider with documented purge and revocation semantics is approved; a scanning position is decided, either by approving a scanner or by an owner-signed decision that scanning is not required for the scoped content; retention durations are set; an admin sign-in implementation is approved; a legal-hold enforcement authority is defined; or alert routing and on-call ownership are assigned. Each of those turns a refusing adapter or an absent authority into a real one, and none of them is a code change first — the adapter boundary is where a provider lands, and the policy ports are where approved terms land.

Mature content needs all of the above *and* its own four, recorded in the [trust and safety freeze report](12-trust-safety-freeze-report.md): an approved Identity provider/policy for depicted-person evidence, approved consent wording and revocation terms, an approved classification taxonomy, and a resolved record-keeping duty under 18 U.S.C. § 2257. Even then the surface question stands separately, because both app stores prohibit the class outright.

The review triggers on [media provider eligibility](../compliance/08-media-provider-eligibility.md) apply: its findings are dated, and an entry whose retrieval date has aged past its trigger is stale evidence that must be re-verified before it supports any decision.

## Status

**PRODUCTION MEDIA PLATFORM CORE: FROZEN**

**LIVE PRODUCTION MEDIA: BLOCKED** — no approved object-storage provider; no approved delivery or CDN provider with documented purge and per-URL revocation semantics; no decided malware and content scanning position; no approved retention durations for quarantined originals, deleted-asset evidence, and legal-hold objects; no approved admin sign-in implementation, which leaves the operator surface unreachable in every environment; no legal-hold enforcement authority; and no alert routing or on-call ownership.

**MATURE CONTENT PRODUCTION ENABLEMENT: BLOCKED** — separately, on its own gates, and unchanged by this milestone.
